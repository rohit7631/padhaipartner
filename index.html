How GPA is calculated

Each grade is worth grade points (A+ and A = 4.0, A− = 3.7, B+ = 3.3, B = 3.0, B− = 2.7, C+ = 2.3, C = 2.0, C− = 1.7, D+ = 1.3, D = 1.0, F = 0). How the Pomodoro technique works

The classic pattern is 25 minutes of focused work, then a 5-minute break, repeated four times before a longer 15–30 minute break. It works because the commitment is small — anyone can focus for 25 minutes — and the regular breaks stop the mental fatigue that makes long, unbroken study sessions unproductive. During a focus session, keep your phone in another room and write down distracting thoughts instead of acting on them; deal with the list in your break.

How to stay inside your essay word limit — without cutting your best ideas

Almost every student has done it: the assignment says 800 words, the draft says 1,140, and the last evening disappears into deleting sentences at random. The essay that comes out is shorter, but it is also worse — because the cuts were panic cuts, not editing. Word limits are not there to punish you. They test whether you can choose what matters. Here is a calmer way to do it.

First, know which kind of limit you have

A hard limit (“800 words maximum, work over the limit will not be marked”) means anything above the number may simply be ignored. A range (“750–850 words”) gives you about 10% either way. A target (“about 800 words”) is guidance. The same draft needs different treatment for each one, so read the instruction before you cut a single line.

Cut in this order — weakest material first

  1. Repeated points. If you made the same argument in the introduction and again in paragraph three, one of them goes. Repetition is the most common reason essays run long.
  2. Long quotations. A six-line quote usually makes a one-line point. Shorten it to the exact phrase you need and put the rest in your own words.
  3. Throat-clearing openings. Sentences like “In today's modern world of the twenty-first century…” say nothing. Deleting them loses no marks.
  4. Extra examples. Two examples prove a point as well as four. Keep the strongest two.
  5. Wordy phrases. “Due to the fact that” is “because”. “In order to” is “to”. A pass through your draft hunting phrases like these often saves 5–8% on its own.

Use reading time as a second opinion

Word count is not the only measure of length. At an average reading speed of 200 words per minute, an 800-word essay takes about four minutes to read — and about six minutes to speak at presentation speed (130 words per minute). If your draft reads aloud for ten minutes when the slot is five, it is too long no matter what the word count says. Timing a read-through catches the dense, heavy paragraphs that a raw count misses.

A simple workflow that works

Write the first draft freely, without watching the count — stopping mid-argument to delete words breaks your thinking. Then paste the draft into the Word Counter, set the limit in the word-goal box, and see exactly how far over you are. Cut in the order above, checking the goal bar after each pass. Most drafts reach the limit in two or three passes, and what survives is the strongest version of the argument, not a random remainder.

Try it: open the Word Counter, paste your draft, and set your limit in the word-goal box — the progress bar shows how much is left to cut.

GPA, CGPA and percentage: how the conversions actually work

Application season produces the same confusion every year: a result card shows a CGPA of 8.4, a form asks for a percentage, and a friend studying abroad talks about a 3.5 GPA as if it were the same thing. They are three different scales, and converting between them is simple — as long as you know which formula belongs to which scale, and where the shortcuts stop being exact.

The 4.0 GPA scale, and why credits matter

On the 4.0 scale, each letter grade carries points: A is 4.0, B is 3.0, C is 2.0, D is 1.0 and F is 0, with plus and minus steps in between (B+ is 3.3, A− is 3.7). Your GPA is not a plain average of those points. Each subject's points are multiplied by its credit hours first. A B in a 4-credit subject pulls your GPA down far more than a B in a 1-credit subject pulls it anywhere — the 4-credit grade counts four times as heavily. That is why two students with the same grades can finish with different GPAs.

Worked example: Mathematics A (4.0 × 4 credits = 16 points), Science B+ (3.3 × 4 = 13.2), English A− (3.7 × 3 = 11.1), Social Studies B (3.0 × 3 = 9). Total points 49.3 ÷ 14 credits = 3.52 GPA.

CGPA to percentage: the × 9.5 rule

Indian boards and many universities convert the 10-point CGPA to percentage with one formula: Percentage = CGPA × 9.5. So a CGPA of 8.4 becomes 8.4 × 9.5 = 79.8%, and the reverse is just as direct: CGPA = Percentage ÷ 9.5, so 79.8% ÷ 9.5 = 8.4. The 9.5 factor exists because the board calibrated it against years of actual subject marks — it is an official approximation, not a mathematical identity.

Where the conversion can mislead you

  • Not every institution uses × 9.5. Some universities use × 10, some publish a conversion table, and some refuse conversion altogether. The figure on your form should use your institution's rule, not a general one.
  • GPA (4.0) and CGPA (10-point) are not interchangeable. A 3.5 GPA is not “8.75 CGPA” by any official formula — the scales are built differently.
  • A converted percentage is an estimate. Two students with the same CGPA can have genuinely different subject marks. For competitive cut-offs, the original CGPA is what counts.

The safe habit: convert for your own planning freely, but before writing a converted figure on any official form, check the handbook or notice that the form itself points to. It takes two minutes and prevents the one error that can get an application rejected.

Percentage problems, solved properly: marks, discounts and percentage change

Percentage is probably the most-used piece of maths in student life — result cards, attendance requirements, scholarship cut-offs, sale discounts, data-interpretation questions. Yet most percentage errors are not calculation errors. They are whole errors: dividing by the wrong total. Get the whole right, and every percentage question becomes the same one-line formula.

The one rule: percent means “out of a hundred”

Percentage = (part ÷ whole) × 100. The whole is the total the part belongs to — the maximum marks, the original price, the starting value. In exam questions, the whole is usually the number that follows the word “of”, or the total the question started from. If you ever feel stuck, write down “part = ?” and “whole = ?” before touching the numbers; the formula then fills itself in.

Type 1 · Marks percentage

Scored 450 out of 500? Percentage = 450 ÷ 500 × 100 = 90%. The same method works backwards. Need 60% to pass a paper of 80 marks? Required marks = 60 ÷ 100 × 80 = 48 marks. And to plan a target: if an exam totals 600 marks and you want 75%, you need 0.75 × 600 = 450 marks overall — which tells you exactly how much room each remaining paper has.

Type 2 · Percent of a number

“What is 15% of 2,400?” is part-hunting: 15 ÷ 100 × 2,400 = 360. Discounts are this type in disguise. A 20% discount on ₹850 removes 0.20 × 850 = ₹170, so you pay 850 − 170 = ₹680. A useful mental shortcut: find 10% first (just move the decimal point one place left), then build — 15% is 10% + 5%, and 5% is half of 10%.

Type 3 · Percentage change — and the percentage-points trap

Percentage change compares a movement against the original value: (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Marks rising from 60 to 75 is (75 − 60) ÷ 60 × 100 = a 25% increase — not 15%. This is where the most common confusion in news and results lives:

  • If your score goes from 60% to 75%, that is 15 percentage points more, but a 25% improvement relative to where you started. Both statements are true; they measure different things.
  • Always divide by the old value for change. Dividing by the new value is the single most frequent percentage-change mistake in exams.
  • Percentages of different wholes cannot be added. 50% of a class of 20 and 50% of a class of 60 are not “100%” of anything.
  • A fall and an equal rise do not cancel out. ₹100 with 20% off becomes ₹80; adding 20% back gives ₹96, not ₹100 — because the second 20% was taken of a smaller whole.

Attendance: the percentage question nobody escapes

Most colleges require 75% attendance. If a subject holds 60 classes, you must attend at least 0.75 × 60 = 45 classes — so you can miss at most 15. Check this early in the term, not in the last week: the classes you have already missed are gone, and every remaining class becomes a larger share of the whole you still control.

Exact age, explained: how years, months and days are counted for forms and exam cut-offs

“I am 17” is good enough for a birthday cake. It is not good enough for an application form. Competitive exams, government jobs, school admissions and sports trials all fix eligibility by exact age on a specific cut-off date — and every year, candidates who were sure they qualified discover they were a few weeks too old, or a few days too young. Exact age is a small calculation with strict rules. Here is how it actually works.

The counting method: completed years, then months, then days

Age counts only what you have completed. You are 17 from your 17th birthday until the day before your 18th — “almost 18” is still 17 on every official form. The standard method, the one used on forms and by the calculator on this page, is:

  1. Count the completed years between the date of birth and the “as on” date.
  2. From the last birthday, count the completed months.
  3. Count the remaining days after the last completed month.

Worked example: date of birth 15 August 2009, age as on 1 January 2027. Completed years: 15 Aug 2009 → 15 Aug 2026 = 17 years. Completed months: 15 Aug → 15 Dec 2026 = 4 months. Remaining days: 15 Dec → 1 Jan = 17 days. Exact age: 17 years, 4 months, 17 days.

Why the days sometimes “borrow” a month

When the “as on” day-of-month is smaller than the birth day-of-month (the 1st versus the 15th above), the subtraction borrows the days of the month just before the “as on” date. That is why the answer depends on real calendar months — 31 days in December, 30 in others, 28 or 29 in February — and why the shortcut of dividing total days by 365 gives a number that is close but often wrong by days or weeks. For an eligibility cut-off, “close” can mean rejected.

Cut-off dates: the part most students misread

  • “Age as on 1 January” (or 1 August, or another fixed date) means your age is frozen on that date — not your age today, and not your age on the exam day. Always calculate against the cut-off date named in the notification.
  • Both ends matter. Many posts set a minimum and a maximum age. Being one day over the maximum on the cut-off date disqualifies, exactly as being one day under the minimum does.
  • Born on 29 February? In non-leap years, different authorities treat the birthday differently (28 February or 1 March). If a cut-off falls near that date, follow the rule in the exam notification itself rather than assuming.
  • Never round. Writing 18 when you are 17 years and 11 months is a wrong entry on a signed form, not a harmless approximation.

The safe habit takes under a minute: find the cut-off date in the official notification, set it as the “as of” date, and read off the years-months-days figure. Do it before you pay any application fee.
